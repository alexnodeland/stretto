# Staged learning replayed on τ²-bench agents

`stretto stage` learns the next version of a deployment's flow as sessions arrive, beside the flow the proxy serves. It scores each new session with both flows before it learns from it, and nothing reaches the proxy until a person commits ([issue #35](https://github.com/alexnodeland/stretto/issues/35), [staged flows](https://stretto.alexnodeland.com/guide/concepts/staged-flows)). This round replays commits along a stream of recorded sessions. For each session it asks which flow would have served it under four ways of committing, and what that flow's lookups would have done.

## Findings

- **A staged flow is not better by default. Committing it every 20 sessions found more of the agent's lookups, and made more detours.** The stream was GLM-5's 160 retail test episodes, under a flow learned from its training episodes:
  - never committed, the flow's lookups were used 1,025 times, with 64 detours;
  - committing the staged flow after every batch of 20: 1,240 used (+21%) and 142 detours (2.2 times as many).

  The relearned flows made lookups at more places. The share the agent used fell from 94% to 90%.
- **Committing only when the staged flow did better on the batch almost never committed.** The rule commits when the staged flow's lookups were used at least as often as the committed flow's, with no more detours. It committed once in 10 orders, and the results stayed about where the first flow left them: 1,032.5 used and 65.9 detours on average.
- **Committing when the staged flow nets more took most of the gain for fewer detours.** The rule commits when used less detours was higher for the staged flow on the batch. It committed about twice per order, the first time after 40 or 60 of the 160 sessions, and reached 1,208 used, 129 detours. Its extra lookups came at the same rate as committing every batch: about 2.8 used for each extra detour.
- **In airline there was nothing to gain.** Over GLM-5's 80 airline test episodes, the first flow's lookups were used 219 times, with 9 detours. Committing every batch gave 221 and 12, and either rule 219 and 10: they committed in four or five of the ten orders.
- **After a new agent took over, relearning did not help, and forgetting only limited the harm.** Claude 3.7 Sonnet's 160 retail test episodes came first, then GPT-4.1-mini's, under a flow learned from Claude 3.7's training episodes. On GPT-4.1-mini's episodes:

  | Committing | Used | Detours |
  |---|---|---|
  | never | 1,172 | 238 |
  | every batch | 1,183 | 272 |
  | every batch, with a half-life of 100 sessions | 1,186 | 253 |

  The staged flow learns from every session so far, and the old agent's sessions outnumbered the new agent's until the end. On Claude 3.7's own sessions, before the splice, committing every batch lost a little: 1,638 used and 211 detours, against 1,671 and 204.
- **The drift alarm is what flags a new agent.** On the same orders, `stretto drift` sounded after the splice in all 10, a median of 8 sessions in (3 to 60). It sounded falsely before the splice in 1 order.

## What it means

The comparison is the thing to commit on, not the calendar. `stretto stage`'s report puts both flows' used lookups and detours side by side, per site, with an interval on each share. On GLM-5's retail stream, a staged flow that nets more is worth a look, and one that only adds lookups at the price of detours is a trade.

Whether that trade pays depends on the domain's costs. At the paper's costs, a saved turn is worth about 2.4 detours in retail ([benchmarks](benchmarks-2026-09-27.md)). The counts here are per decision, not turns saved, so they bound the trade rather than price it.

After a change of agent, the alarm is the signal. Learn a fresh flow from the new agent's sessions, or from all of them with a half-life ([drift](drift-2026-09-28.md)), and review it before it is committed.

## Setup

- **Agents and domains:**
  - GLM-5's four trials of every task, retail and airline, in order;
  - for the splice, Claude 3.7 Sonnet's retail episodes, then GPT-4.1-mini's, from τ²-bench's own results.
- **The first committed flow** is learned from the agent's episodes on the training tasks (`stretto learn --habit-only --results`).
- **The stream** is the training episodes, then the test episodes in a random order (10 orders), 20 at a time.
  - After each batch, `stretto stage --results --decider reach --window 20` scores the batch with both flows, then learns the staged flow again from every episode so far.
  - Each batch is scored by the staged flow as it was before the batch, so committing after every batch needs no second run: that run's own scores are the ones it would have had.
- **The four ways of committing:**
  - never;
  - after every batch;
  - after a batch where the staged flow dominated: used at least as often, no more detours, one of the two strictly;
  - after a batch where it netted more: used less detours higher than the committed flow's.

  The last two run `stretto flow-commit` when the rule holds. Every result is over the test episodes, as the flow then committed served them.
- **The splice** runs never and every batch, the latter also with `--half-life 100`. `stretto drift` watches the same orders under the first flow.
- **Scoring**, as `stretto promote` scores a flow:
  - After each of the agent's calls, the lookups the flow would have made there. A lookup is used when the agent made it in a later LLM turn, and a detour when it never did.
  - A lookup a flow would make after several of the agent's calls counts after each. So the totals count decisions, not calls spared, and they compare ways of committing like for like.
  - The episodes were recorded without a flow, so nothing was served, and the scores are exact.

## What it does not show

- **Counts per decision overstate the calls spared.** They compare ways of committing like for like, but they are not turns saved, which [the replays](reach-2026-09-26.md) measure.
- **Twenty sessions is a small batch to decide on.** The rules looked at the last batch alone, whose intervals are wide. A deployment can compare over more (`--window`), and the report's intervals say how far apart the flows really are.
- **The flows used the reach decider at 0.3, with no arbiter.** An arbiter carried over from the committed flow is not relearned, and was not tried.
- **Benchmark tasks in a random order are steadier than traffic.** A deployment's sessions come in runs of one kind, and a staged flow learned in such a run leans toward it.

## Reproduce

```bash
cargo build --release -p stretto-report
python3 scripts/staged_study.py --tau2 PATH/TO/tau2-bench --glm5 .data/tau2-targets \
    --stretto target/release/stretto --orders 10 --batch 20 --jobs 4 \
    --out docs/results/staged-2026-09-28.json
```

It took about 27 minutes, four runs at a time (`--jobs 4`). [staged-2026-09-28.json](staged-2026-09-28.json) holds every order's row: each way of committing's lookups, used, served and detours, the batches after which the rules committed, and the alarm's delay.
